# Narrative, public copy, and release workflow

## Narrative analysis

Do not summarize the source. Identify:

1. Main attributable claim and implied audience conclusion.
2. Evidence, sources, comparisons, and missing context.
3. Beneficiary, disadvantaged party, and assumed standard of judgment.
4. Timing, format, distribution, and whether repetition is independently verified.
5. Effect on trust, category expectations, partner decisions, regulatory context, or explanation cost.
6. Recommendation: no action, monitor, correct facts, build an alternative evidence base, engage selectively, or escalate.

Quote only the minimum necessary text and preserve context. Label motive or coordination claims as unverified unless supported.

## Public-context copy review

Review copy in this order:

1. State intended audience, objective, channel, occasion, and call to action.
2. Extract every factual, comparative, performance, credential, and social-proof claim.
3. Test literal meaning, foreseeable ambiguity, excluded audience interpretation, cropped screenshot, headline, hostile reading, and long-term precedent.
4. Check identity, dignity, vulnerability, tragedy, health, safety, minors, family, labor, class, region, culture, and public-interest sensitivity where relevant.
5. Decide: publish, revise before publishing, or change the underlying concept.
6. Preserve the legitimate objective while removing unsupported or needlessly harmful framing.

Do not treat keywords as proof of risk. Explain the mechanism, affected stakeholder, likelihood, impact, and mitigation.

## Press release workflow

### Intake gate

Confirm who did what, when and where, what changed for whom, the communication objective, target media/audience, and proof available. If the central action is not supported, do not draft a finished release.

### News value

Choose one lead: verified result, new product/service, partnership outcome, research finding, public-interest action, event outcome, or accountable next step. An event itself is rarely the news; identify what was announced, changed, measured, or made available.

### Structure

1. Headline with actor, action, and meaningful result/scope.
2. Lead with essential facts and audience relevance.
3. Evidence and mechanism explaining what changed.
4. Context necessary to interpret the facts.
5. Attributable quote only when it adds a decision, explanation, boundary, or next step.
6. Limitations, availability, eligibility, methodology, or next milestone.
7. Boilerplate and contact information when requested.

### Editing rules

- Prefer concrete actions, data, scope, and mechanisms over self-description.
- Keep plans, pilots, launches, and results in their correct status.
- Preserve measurement definitions, date ranges, comparison bases, and sample limits.
- Do not invent dates, quotes, credentials, endorsements, policies, customer stories, or market conclusions.
- Remove unsupported superlatives, vague corporate jargon, manufactured consensus, and promotional claims disguised as third-party reporting.
- When asked to imitate a publication, analyze user-supplied samples or current examples; do not claim an exact editorial voice from memory.

## Delivery formats

For a review, provide verdict, objective, evidence gaps, material risks, stakeholder readings, screenshot/headline test, recommended edits, and final revised copy. For a release, provide the finished draft plus a short evidence/approval checklist; do not expose internal chain-of-thought or hidden scoring.
